Quick Answer

Buy or compare travel insurance as soon as you make your first nonrefundable trip payment, not when a hurricane appears on the forecast. Once a tropical storm is named or otherwise becomes a known event, a newly purchased policy will typically exclude losses connected to that storm, so the useful window may already be closed.

ATLAS Letters — Miji to Suo: Suo, I found a good coastal hotel for late summer, but the rate becomes nonrefundable today. Should I insure the trip now, wait for the seasonal forecast, or buy only if a storm starts moving toward the destination?

Suo’s reply: Treat the first nonrefundable payment as the decision point. First confirm what the airline, hotel, cruise line, and credit card already promise. Then compare a policy while the trip is still free of a known storm that could affect it.

The Money at Risk

The amount at risk is the portion of your trip that you cannot recover from the travel supplier. Add the nonrefundable airfare, hotel deposits, cruise fare, vacation rental, tours, transfers, event tickets, and prepaid activities. Do not insure a refundable amount simply because it appears on the booking total. Keep the cancellation terms that were shown when you paid.

Hurricane-season disruption can create several different losses. A carrier may cancel a flight, a hotel may remain open while the airport closes, a cruise may change ports, or you may reach the destination and then have to leave early. One refund rule or one insurance benefit does not automatically solve all four situations.

The U.S. Department of Transportation says a passenger is entitled to a refund when an airline cancels a flight and the passenger chooses not to travel or accept alternative compensation. A refund may also be due after a significant schedule change when the traveler declines the changed itinerary. That protection concerns the airline ticket and certain airline fees; it does not automatically repay unrelated lodging, tours, meals, ground transport, or the unused part of a trip already underway.

The National Association of Insurance Commissioners advises that travel insurance is designed for sudden and unforeseen events. It specifically warns that buying after a winter or tropical storm is named typically will not provide coverage for claims related to that event. This is why waiting for a threatening forecast can turn a policy purchase into a payment for protection that does not address the storm you are worried about.

The key distinction is between a bad forecast and a covered loss. A standard policy does not usually pay simply because you would rather avoid uncertain weather. The contract may instead require a specified event such as a carrier cancellation, mandatory evacuation, destination becoming uninhabitable, or a defined delay. The wording and timing control.

What to Do Before You Travel

  1. List the first nonrefundable date. Put every airfare, lodging, cruise, tour, transfer, and activity into a simple worksheet. Record the amount, refund deadline, and cancellation rule. This establishes when your financial exposure actually begins.
  2. Compare coverage immediately after that exposure begins. Do not wait for a seasonal outlook or a storm track. Early comparison preserves more options and gives you time to read the policy before a named event limits storm-related coverage.
  3. Separate supplier refunds from insurance. Save the airline contract of carriage, hotel rate terms, cruise policy, and tour cancellation terms. Ask what happens after a named storm, airport closure, evacuation order, shortened cruise, or destination closure. A supplier credit is not the same as a cash refund, and accepting it may affect another claim.
  4. Read the severe-weather definitions. Search the policy for hurricane, named storm, severe weather, natural disaster, mandatory evacuation, uninhabitable, common carrier, trip cancellation, trip interruption, delay, and foreseeable event. Do not rely on a sales-page summary.
  5. Check the timing rules. Confirm when coverage becomes effective, whether benefits tied to the first trip payment have an eligibility window, and whether all prepaid trip costs must be insured. Optional CFAR and some waivers can have stricter deadlines than standard benefits.
  6. Match coverage to the real failure point. If the largest risk is a cruise deposit, verify itinerary-change and embarkation terms. If it is a vacation rental, verify what happens when access is blocked but the building is undamaged. If it is an international trip, separately check emergency medical and evacuation benefits because trip cancellation insurance usually does not pay foreign medical bills.
  7. Keep evidence before a storm exists. Save dated booking confirmations, payment receipts, policy certificate, full policy wording, and screenshots or PDFs of cancellation terms. If disruption occurs, save carrier notices, official evacuation orders, closure notices, delay records, new travel receipts, and refund correspondence.
  8. Know the first phone call. Save the insurer’s assistance and claim numbers offline. When disruption begins, contact the carrier or supplier for its remedy, then contact the insurer before making expensive alternative arrangements when it is safe and practical to do so.
  9. Monitor official information. NOAA says the Atlantic and central Pacific hurricane seasons officially run from June 1 through November 30. Use National Hurricane Center outlooks and destination emergency agencies for decisions; social posts and model screenshots are not substitutes for official notices required by a claim.
  10. Make a go, change, or cancel decision sheet. Write who can cancel each reservation, the last refundable moment, what evidence is required, and which expenses need prior approval. This converts a vague weather worry into actions that can be taken quickly.

A practical buying script is: “My first nonrefundable payment was made on this date. Which hurricane or severe-weather events are covered, when does coverage start, what makes an event foreseeable, and what documents would prove cancellation, interruption, or delay?” Save the answer with the policy version you reviewed.

  • First nonrefundable payment date and amount
  • Supplier cancellation and refund deadlines
  • Full policy wording, not only a benefit summary
  • Named-storm and foreseeable-event definition
  • Covered cancellation, interruption, and delay triggers
  • CFAR eligibility and timing rules, if considered
  • Airline refund and rebooking decision recorded separately
  • Offline assistance numbers and official weather sources
  • Folder for notices, receipts, refund records, and claim evidence

Common Mistakes

Waiting until a storm is named. The consequence is that the event may already be foreseeable and excluded from a newly purchased policy. Compare coverage when the first payment becomes nonrefundable.

Assuming every hurricane-related loss is covered. The consequence is a denied claim when the policy requires a specific trigger that never occurred. Match the facts to the contract’s listed covered reasons.

Insuring refundable expenses. The consequence can be paying to insure money the supplier would already return. Calculate only the genuine nonrefundable exposure and update it as additional payments are made.

Treating CFAR as ordinary cancellation coverage. The consequence is missing a purchase deadline, cancellation cutoff, or trip-cost requirement. Review CFAR as a separate optional benefit with separate rules.

Confusing an airline refund with complete trip reimbursement. The consequence is leaving hotel, cruise, rental, or tour losses unresolved. Handle the carrier remedy first, then assess each remaining prepaid expense.

Canceling based only on an alarming forecast. The consequence may be a voluntary cancellation that does not meet a covered reason. Check official notices, supplier options, and the policy before acting.

Discarding booking terms after purchase. The consequence is weak proof of what was nonrefundable and when. Preserve the terms, payment record, policy version, notices, and all refund decisions.

FAQ

Should I buy travel insurance before hurricane season starts?

The calendar is less important than your booking date. Compare coverage as soon as you make the first nonrefundable payment, and buy before a storm that could affect the trip becomes a known or named event.

Will a policy cover a hurricane that has already been named?

Often not for losses connected to that storm. NAIC warns that buying after a tropical storm is named typically will not cover claims related to that event. Read the policy definition of a foreseeable or known event.

Does trip cancellation coverage apply whenever rain ruins a vacation?

No. Standard coverage generally requires a listed covered reason, such as a qualifying carrier cancellation, destination evacuation, or severe weather condition defined by the contract. Disappointment or a poor forecast alone is usually insufficient.

Do I need insurance if the airline must refund a canceled flight?

Possibly. A DOT refund may return the unused airfare when you decline rebooking, but it does not automatically reimburse a separate prepaid hotel, cruise, rental, tour, extra meals, or an interruption after departure.

Is Cancel For Any Reason coverage the same as regular trip cancellation?

No. CFAR is an optional, time-sensitive benefit with its own purchase window, cancellation deadline, eligibility rules, and partial-reimbursement terms. The exact contract controls, so compare it separately from standard named-reason coverage.
