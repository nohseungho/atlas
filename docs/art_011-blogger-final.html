Quick Answer

If you are over 65 and traveling internationally, treat your U.S. health coverage and travel medical insurance as two separate layers: Original Medicare usually does not cover care outside the United States, while Medigap and Medicare Advantage travel benefits can be limited. Before departure, confirm emergency medical care, medical evacuation, pre-existing-condition rules, age restrictions, payment procedures, and the exact destinations covered. Compare the policy wording—not just the headline benefit—and keep the assistance number and proof of coverage with you.

The Money at Risk

The largest financial gap is often not a routine clinic visit. The U.S. Department of State says an air-ambulance evacuation back to the United States can cost $20,000 to $200,000, depending on the traveler’s location and medical condition. It also warns that the U.S. government does not pay a citizen’s medical bills abroad and that many facilities require payment or a deposit before treatment. That means a traveler may need available cash or credit even when a policy later reimburses an eligible claim. See the State Department’s current guidance on medicine and health abroad.

Original Medicare usually leaves the traveler responsible for the full cost outside the United States, except in a few narrow situations. Medicare.gov lists examples such as a foreign hospital being closer than an appropriate U.S. hospital during an emergency in the United States, or certain direct travel through Canada between Alaska and another state. It also notes that Medicare drug plans do not cover prescription drugs purchased outside the country. Those exceptions are too specific to serve as a general international travel plan.

A qualifying Medigap plan may reduce the gap, but its foreign-travel benefit is not unlimited. The April 2026 Medicare publication says most applicable Medigap plans pay 80% of billed charges for certain medically necessary emergency care after a $250 yearly deductible, only when the emergency begins during the first 60 days of the trip, and with a $50,000 lifetime limit. A traveler who has used part of that lifetime benefit may have less remaining. The insurer should confirm the remaining amount and whether the planned trip falls within the policy terms.

Medicare Advantage works differently because benefits vary by plan. Medicare explains that a plan may cover additional services outside the United States, but the traveler must check directly with the plan. “Emergency” and “urgently needed” care may also be defined differently from follow-up care, routine treatment, rehabilitation, or transport home.

The policy price is only one side of the decision. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners says travel insurance policies usually cost 5% to 10% of the total trip price, with cost affected by coverage, season, destination, age, and other factors. That figure describes travel insurance generally; it is not a quote for a particular senior traveler or proof that a policy includes medical care. A low price can reflect narrower benefits, higher deductibles, secondary reimbursement, or exclusions that matter more than the premium.

What to Do Before You Travel

  1. Map your current coverage first. Write down whether you have Original Medicare, a Medigap plan, Medicare Advantage, retiree coverage, employer coverage, or credit-card benefits. Call each relevant plan and ask about every destination and the full travel dates. Ask separately about emergency treatment, urgent treatment, hospitalization, prescriptions, follow-up care, medical evacuation, repatriation, and care on a cruise ship.
  2. Get the answer in a form you can keep. Save the current Evidence of Coverage, policy certificate, or written response. Record the date, the representative’s name or reference number, and the exact section discussed. A verbal “you should be fine” is not a substitute for policy language.
  3. List the trip-specific risks. Consider the quality and distance of medical facilities, rural travel, cruises, altitude, heat, planned activities, and every country in transit. The CDC says travel health and evacuation coverage can be particularly useful for people over 65 and travelers with pre-existing conditions. It also advises asking whether an insurer can arrange direct hospital payment and provide a physician-backed support center around the clock.
  4. Compare medical and trip protection separately. Trip cancellation coverage may protect prepaid travel costs without paying foreign medical bills. Travel medical coverage may address eligible treatment without protecting the trip investment. If these distinctions are unclear, use the ATLAS comparison of travel medical insurance versus trip insurance before reviewing quotes.
  5. Read the definitions and exclusions. Search the contract for pre-existing condition, look-back period, medically necessary, emergency, stable, acute onset, evacuation, nearest appropriate facility, return to home country, high-risk activity, war, civil unrest, pandemic, and age. Do not assume a phrase has its everyday meaning. Ask what documentation is required and who decides whether evacuation is medically necessary.
  6. Check purchase-timing requirements. Some benefits or waivers depend on when coverage is purchased relative to the first trip payment or another event. The exact deadline is policy-specific, so obtain it from the current contract rather than relying on a general online rule. Never expect a policy purchased after a known illness, named storm, or other foreseeable event to cover that event unless the wording clearly says so.
  7. Plan for payment at the point of care. Ask whether the insurer can guarantee direct payment, whether you must pay first, what cards or cash facilities at the destination accept, and how reimbursement works. The CDC advises travelers to keep all medical records, itemized charges, and receipts. Store secure digital copies that a trusted contact can access.
  8. Prepare medical information and medicines. The State Department recommends carrying medicines in original packaging, bringing enough for the trip plus extra for delays, taking copies of prescriptions, and asking a doctor for a letter listing conditions and generic medication names. Confirm destination rules because a medicine legal in the United States can be restricted elsewhere. Pair this step with the ATLAS guide to building a travel health kit.
  9. Create a one-page emergency plan. Include policy numbers, assistance and claims lines, emergency contacts, allergies, medications, treating physicians, and the nearest suitable facilities. Share it with a travel companion and a trusted person at home. Know the immediate sequence for getting care; ATLAS also explains what to do if you get sick abroad.

Common Mistakes

Assuming Medicare follows you everywhere. The consequence can be responsibility for the full foreign medical bill. Start with Medicare’s rule that overseas coverage is generally unavailable, then prove whether an exception or supplemental benefit applies.

Buying trip cancellation coverage and believing it is health insurance. The consequence is a protected airfare or hotel deposit but no benefit for eligible treatment abroad. Confirm that the policy schedule names emergency medical treatment and evacuation if those are the risks you intend to transfer.

Looking only at the maximum benefit. The consequence can be a denied or reduced claim despite a large number on the sales page. Deductibles, coinsurance, secondary payment, exclusions, sublimits, and the definition of medically necessary care determine how the maximum works.

Ignoring age and pre-existing-condition language. The consequence can be a policy that excludes the condition most likely to require care. The CDC advises older travelers, especially those with underlying conditions, to research specialized coverage early and discuss concerns with the insurer before departure.

Assuming “evacuation” means a flight home. The consequence may be transport only to the nearest facility the insurer considers appropriate. Read who chooses the destination, what medical approval is required, and whether later transportation to the United States is included.

Waiting until a risk becomes known. The consequence can be exclusion of the exact event causing concern. NAIC notes, for example, that buying after a storm is named will likely not cover claims related to it. Apply the same discipline to illness, destination events, and other foreseeable circumstances by reading the policy’s effective-date and known-event language.

Traveling without a documentation plan. The consequence can be delayed reimbursement or an incomplete claim. Keep clinical notes, diagnoses, itemized invoices, receipts, proof of payment, and records of calls with the assistance service.

FAQ

Does Medicare cover medical care during international travel?

Original Medicare usually does not cover health care outside the United States. Medicare lists only limited exceptions involving certain emergencies near a foreign hospital, qualifying direct travel through Canada, some residents near a foreign hospital, and specific cruise-ship circumstances. Confirm your exact situation before relying on an exception.

Does Medigap cover foreign travel emergencies after age 65?

Some Medigap plans cover certain medically necessary emergency care abroad. Medicare’s April 2026 publication describes an 80% benefit after a $250 yearly deductible, a $50,000 lifetime limit, and a requirement that the emergency begin during the first 60 days of the trip. Confirm your plan letter, remaining lifetime benefit, and all exclusions.

Is travel medical insurance the same as trip insurance?

No. Travel medical coverage focuses on eligible short-term health care abroad, while trip cancellation or interruption coverage focuses on eligible prepaid travel losses. A package may combine them, but the presence of one benefit does not prove the other is included.

How much travel medical coverage should a senior traveler choose?

There is no universal amount. Compare the policy’s medical and evacuation limits with the itinerary, destination facilities, existing coverage, health needs, and the State Department’s warning that air-ambulance evacuation to the United States can cost $20,000 to $200,000. Also examine deductibles, sublimits, and exclusions rather than choosing by the maximum alone.

Can a senior travel policy cover a pre-existing condition?

It depends on the policy. Some contracts exclude pre-existing conditions; others may offer a waiver or define a limited acute onset benefit if stated requirements are met. Ask the insurer to identify the applicable definition, review period, stability rule, purchase deadline, age rule, and required records in the contract.
